# Meeting 9: Footnotes, Quotations, and Bibliography

These notes are based on my lecturer's explanation in the ninth meeting (21 November 2026), a review of footnotes, quotations, and bibliographies.

## 1. Learning Objectives

- Students understand the concepts of footnotes, quotations, and a bibliography.
- Students are able to prepare footnotes, quotations, and a bibliography.

## 2. Footnotes

### 2.1 Definition

A footnote is additional information about parts of a scientific text, placed at the foot (bottom) of the page. If this information is placed at the end of the whole text instead, it is called an **endnote**.

In essence, footnotes are about borrowing: the author shows that an idea or sentence was borrowed from another source.

### 2.2 Function

A footnote gives clarification or supporting sources without interrupting the flow of the main text, so readers can get deeper information if they want it. Footnotes are often used in academic works, nonfiction books, and scientific articles.

### 2.3 Purpose

To serve as proof that a statement or quotation in the text really comes from a source that can be traced.

### 2.4 How to Write a Footnote

- Write the author's full name, not inverted.
- Put a comma between the author's name and the book title.
- After the title, give the publication details in parentheses: city, publisher, and year, followed by the page number.

**Pattern for a book:**

> Author Name, *Book Title* (City: Publisher, Year), p. page number.

### 2.5 Footnote Examples

> **Note:** the sample works below come from the lecture. Their titles are translated into English for readability.

**a. From a book**

Sentence in the text:

> "A sentence is the smallest unit of speech or text that expresses a complete thought."

Footnote:

1. Ida Saidah, *Smart at Writing Scientific Papers* (Surabaya: Madiaguru, 2018), p. 11.

**b. From an article in a collection of writings**

Pattern:

> Author Name, "Article Title," in *Collection Title*, ed. Editor Name (City: Publisher, Year), p. page number.

Sentence in the text:

> "When responding to an event, literate people put thinking ahead of feelings."

Footnote:

1. Ida Saidah et al., "The Key to Educational Success," (Bekasi: Jendela Puspita, 2025), p. 83.

The abbreviation **et al.** (and others) is generally used when a work has more than three authors.

**c. From a seminar paper**

Pattern:

> Author Name, "Paper Title," paper presented at Seminar Name, City, date.

Sentence in the text:

> "Management is very important in carrying out activities."

Footnote:

1. Mochmoed Efendhie, "Archives as a Source of Information in Decision Making," paper presented at [seminar name], [city], [date].

### 2.6 Abbreviations in Footnotes

Some style guides, including those used at many universities, still use the following Latin abbreviations for repeated sources:

| Abbreviation | Meaning | Used when |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Ibid.** | in the same place | The source is the same as the footnote immediately before, but the page is different. Example: *Ibid., p. 12.* |
| **Op. cit.** | in the work already cited | The source was cited before, but another source comes in between, and the page is different. |
| **Loc. cit.** | at the place already cited | The source was cited before, another source comes in between, and the page is the same. |

> **Note:** these rules differ between style guides. Follow the guide of your university or the journal you are writing for.

## 3. Quotations

### 3.1 Definition

According to Saidah (2018:49), a quotation is a borrowed sentence or paragraph published in print or electronic media.

### 3.2 Purpose

- To support the argument the author puts forward.
- To provide convincing evidence and show that a statement is based on earlier research.
- To trace the sources of information used.
- To make further research easier.
- To broaden the reader's understanding of the topic discussed.

### 3.3 Types of Quotations

- **Direct quotation**: the words are exactly the same as in the source.
- **Indirect quotation**: only the essence is taken, meaning it is summarized from several sentences or paragraphs and written in the author's own words.

### 3.4 Principles of Quoting

1. Do not make any changes.
2. Do not correct mistakes. If the original source contains an error, the author may add **[sic]** after the word to show that the error comes from the source.
3. A quotation may be shortened with an **ellipsis** (omitting part of a sentence, several words, or several sentences):
   - In the middle of a sentence, the ellipsis is **three dots**.
   - At the end of a sentence, the ellipsis is **four dots** (three ellipsis dots plus the full stop).

**Example (illustration):**

- Original sentence: "A scientific work is writing prepared from data and facts and is objective."
- Ellipsis in the middle: "A scientific work is writing ... and is objective."
- Ellipsis at the end: "A scientific work is writing prepared from data and facts...."

### 3.5 How to Quote

**a. Direct quotation of fewer than four lines**

- Integrated into the text.
- Double line spacing.
- Enclosed in quotation marks ("...").

**b. Direct quotation of more than four lines**

- Separated from the text with 2.5 line spacing.
- Typed in single spacing and indented, without quotation marks.

**c. Indirect quotation**

- Integrated into the text.
- Not enclosed in quotation marks, because it is written in the author's own words. The source must still be named.

> **Note:** the four-line limit and spacing details can differ between style guides. Follow the guide of your university or the journal you are writing for.

## 4. Bibliography

### 4.1 Definition

A bibliography is a collection of references used in a written work, containing complete information about those sources.

### 4.2 Types of Bibliography

- **Numeric**: each source is given a serial number and is cited in the text using that number.
- **Alphabetical**: arranged alphabetically by the authors' last names.
- **Thematic**: grouped by topic or subject.

### 4.3 Functions

- Avoiding plagiarism.
- Strengthening arguments.
- Giving credit to the original authors.
- Helping readers find additional sources.

### 4.4 Elements of a Bibliography Entry

1. Author's name
2. Year of publication
3. Title of the book, article, or journal
4. Publisher
5. City of publication
6. URL (for online sources)

### 4.5 Bibliography Formats

**Book:**

> Author's last name, First name. (Year of publication). *Book title*. City of publication: Publisher.

Example:

> Saidah, Ida. (2018). *Smart at Writing Scientific Papers*. Surabaya: Madiaguru.

**Journal article:**

> Author's last name, First name. (Year of publication). Article title. *Journal Title*, volume(issue), pages.

### 4.6 How to Compile a Bibliography

1. Collect all the sources used.
2. Sort them alphabetically by the authors' last names.
3. Follow the formatting rules of the citation style being used.
4. Use reference management software (if available).

Also make sure every source cited in the text appears in the bibliography, and vice versa.

### 4.7 Reference Management Software

- **Zotero**: free, open-source, and very flexible.
- **Mendeley**: free for the basic version, with good collaboration features.
- **EndNote**: paid, but with a very complete set of features.

### 4.8 Bibliography Styles

| Style | Short description |
| --- | --- |
| **APA** | Uses an author-date system. Common in psychology, education, and the social sciences. |
| **MLA** | Uses an author-page system. Common in literature and the humanities. |
| **IEEE** | Uses serial numbers in square brackets, for example [1]. Common in engineering and informatics. |
| **Chicago** | Uses footnotes and a bibliography. Common in history and publishing. |
