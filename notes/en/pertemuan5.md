# Lecture 5: Sentences and Effective Sentences

These notes are taken from the lecturer's explanation in the fifth lecture.

## 1. Learning Outcomes

Students are able to explain:

- The definition of a sentence
- Types of sentences
- Parts of a sentence
- Sentence patterns
- Effective sentences
- Reasoning

and are skilled in writing effective sentences.

## 2. Definition of a Sentence

A sentence is a combination of words that expresses an intention with a complete thought and complete information. A sentence in the formal register, whether spoken or written, must have a subject (the topic of the sentence) and a predicate (what is said about it).

A sentence is not merely a combination of words, but a piece of information conveyed to the listener or reader.

## 3. Two Kinds of Sentences Based on the Predicate

1. Sentences whose predicate is a verb.
2. Sentences whose predicate is not a verb.

**Examples (sentences with a verb predicate):**

- Passive sentence: *Tugas itu dikerjakan oleh mahasiswa.* (The assignment was done by the students.)
- Active sentence: *Mahasiswa mengerjakan tugas itu.* (The students did the assignment.)

## 4. Basic Sentence Patterns

- N + V (noun + verb)
- N + Adj (noun + adjective)
- N + Num (noun + numeral)

## 5. Types of Sentences by Grammatical Structure

These types are identified based on subject, predicate, object, and adverbial.

**Example:**

> (*Para Mahasiswa*) (*Sedang Belajar*) — (The students) (are studying)
> Subject — Predicate

Notes on adverbials:

- An adverbial may consist of more than one word.
- An adverbial may be placed at the beginning or the end of a sentence, but not in the middle.

### 5.1 Simple Sentences (Simplex)

A simple sentence consists of one subject and one predicate.

### 5.2 Compound Sentences (Complex)

A compound sentence is formed by combining two or more simple sentences, and is grouped into four types. The purpose of forming a compound or complex sentence is to make the sentence effective.

**a. Additive compound sentences**

Two or more simple sentences are joined with *dan* ("and") or *serta* ("as well as") if the two sentences are in line with each other.

- Example: *Kami membaca dan mereka menulis.* (We read and they write.)
- If more than two simple sentences are joined, commas may be used. Example: *Direktur senang, karyawan duduk teratur, dan para nasabah online.* (The director is pleased, the staff are seated in order, and the customers are online.)

**b. Contrastive compound sentences**

Two simple sentences are joined with *tetapi* ("but") if the sentences show contrast.

- Example: *Adiknya tinggi tetapi kakaknya pendek.* (The younger sibling is tall but the older sibling is short.)
- Another conjunction that can be used is *melainkan* ("but rather"). Example: *Ia bukan peneliti melainkan dosen luar biasa.* (He is not a researcher but rather an adjunct lecturer.)

**c. Sequential compound sentences**

Two or more simple sentences are joined with *lalu* or *kemudian* ("then") if the events described occur in sequence.

- Example: *Mula-mula disebutkan nama-nama juara menyanyi tingkat remaja, kemudian disebutkan nama-nama juara menyanyi tingkat dewasa.* (First the names of the teen singing champions were announced, then the names of the adult singing champions were announced.)

**d. Condensed compound sentences**

A sentence form that condenses two or more simple sentences by merging one of their shared elements.

- Example: *"Kami berlatih, kami bertanding, dan kami berhasil menang"* (We trained, we competed, and we managed to win) is condensed into *"Kami berlatih, bertanding, dan berhasil menang."* (We trained, competed, and managed to win.)

### 5.3 Subordinate Compound Sentences

A subordinate compound sentence consists of one independent clause (the main clause) and one or more dependent clauses (subordinate clauses). The core idea is placed in the main clause, while its relation in terms of time, cause, effect, purpose, condition, and so on is expressed in the subordinate clause.

**Examples:**

- *Para pemain sudah lelah.* (The players are tired.)
- *Para pemain boleh istirahat.* (The players may rest.)
- *Karena para pemain sudah lelah, para pemain boleh beristirahat.* (Because the players are tired, the players may rest.)
- *Ketika suhu ruang kuliah terasa sangat dingin, mahasiswa mematikan AC.* (When the lecture room felt very cold, the students turned off the air conditioner.)

### 5.4 Mixed Compound Sentences

A mixed compound sentence consists of a subordinate (complex) compound sentence and a coordinate compound sentence. This form usually appears in spoken language.

**Examples:**

- *Karena hari sudah malam, kami berhenti dan langsung pulang.* (Because it was already night, we stopped and went straight home.)
- *Kami pulang, tetapi mereka masih bekerja karena tugasnya belum selesai.* (We went home, but they were still working because their task was not yet finished.)

## 6. Types of Sentences by Rhetorical Style

**Loose sentences**

If a sentence is arranged beginning with the main element (main clause) followed by the additional element (subordinate clause), this style of presentation is called loose.

**Periodic (climactic) sentences**

If a sentence is arranged beginning with the subordinate clause followed by the main clause, this style of presentation is called periodic. The reader cannot yet understand the sentence after reading only the subordinate clause, and only grasps its meaning after reading the main clause. Before the sentence ends, there is a sense that something is still awaited (the climactic main clause).

**Balanced sentences**

If a sentence is arranged as a coordinate or mixed compound sentence, this style of presentation is called balanced when its structure shows parallel, aligned elements set into a symmetrical sentence construction.

- Example: *Jika stabilitas nasional mantap, masyarakat dapat bekerja dengan tenang dan dapat beribadah dengan leluasa.* (If national stability is firm, the public can work in peace and can worship freely.)

## 7. Types of Sentences by Function

- **Declarative sentences (statements)**: used when the speaker wants to state something fully while conveying information to the listener.
- **Interrogative sentences (questions)**: used when the speaker wants to obtain information or an expected reaction (an answer); usually with a falling intonation and a question mark.
- **Imperative sentences (commands and requests)**: used when the speaker wants to order or request something.
- **Exclamatory sentences**: used when the speaker wants to express a strong or sudden feeling.

## 8. Words, Phrases, Clauses, and Sentences

- **Word**: the smallest unit of language.
- **Phrase**: a linguistic unit that is potentially a combination of two or more words, but does not have the characteristics of a clause.

## 9. Effective Sentences

**Definition:** a sentence that is able to convey an idea or information accurately, clearly, and in a way that is easily understood by the reader or listener.

**Purposes of effective sentences:**

- So that the sentence is understood.
- To convey information clearly.
- To avoid misunderstanding.

**Characteristics of effective sentences:**

- Uses the right words.
- Not long-winded.
- Easy to understand.
- Has a clear structure.
- Has no double meaning (not ambiguous).

**Requirements of effective sentences:**

- Unity of idea.
- Economy of words.
- Accuracy of words.
- Logic of the sentence.

A sentence must convey one main idea clearly.

### Opinion Piece: AI Data Center in Jatiluhur, Purwakarta

#### Introduction

I completely reject the construction of the AI data center in Jatiluhur, Purwakarta.

On 22 September 2026, West Java Governor Dedi Mulyadi inaugurated the start of construction of AI Campus 2, or CGK4, owned by BDx Data Centers. The site is designed for a total capacity of 640 MW across 6 buildings, with the first building at 120 MW targeted to begin operating in early 2027. It is located only about 5 kilometers from the Jatiluhur Dam, the main source of water and electricity for West Java for decades.

In my view, this project places the needs of the technology industry above the basic needs of residents and farmers. There are five main reasons: electricity, water amid drought, the fate of farmers, the impact on nearby residents, and economic benefits that fall short of what was promised.

#### Reasons for Rejection

##### 1. Its Electricity Demand Far Exceeds Jatiluhur's Capacity

The electricity demand of this site (640 MW) is more than three times the capacity of the Jatiluhur hydroelectric plant, which is only about 186 MW. This means that even if all of the dam's electricity were diverted to the data center, its needs would still not be met.

BDx has only just begun planning to explore the use of hydropower together with Perum Jasa Tirta II. However, Jatiluhur's electricity has long flowed into the Java-Bali grid for households and other industries. If part of it is diverted, the shortfall must be covered from other sources, which in Java still largely come from coal-fired power plants.

During the dry season, the reservoir's water flow drops, so the hydroelectric plant's output also declines. Under such conditions, the data center and the public would be competing for the same electricity.

##### 2. Water for Rice Fields Is Threatened Amid Drought

An AI data center needs not only electricity but also large amounts of water to cool its servers. Depending on the cooling technology, a large-scale data center can consume millions of liters of water per day.

The Jatiluhur Reservoir was built from the outset to irrigate more than 240,000 hectares of rice fields in Karawang, Subang, Bekasi, and the surrounding areas. West Java is also among the three largest rice-producing provinces in Indonesia. If the reservoir's water is shared with a data center, farmers will be the first to feel the shortage, and the impact could reach national rice prices.

The timing is also wrong. According to reports from BMKG, NOAA, WMO, and Reuters up to October 2026, Indonesia is facing a serious drought worsened by El Niño, and the risk of reduced rainfall remains high into early 2027. That coincides with the target operating date of the first building.

Law No. 17 of 2019 on Water Resources already stipulates that basic daily needs and smallholder agriculture must be given priority. Therefore, Jatiluhur's water should remain the right of farmers and residents, not an industrial commodity.

##### 3. Nearby Residents Bear the Impact

Experience in the United States shows that residents living near data centers are often the ones most harmed. In Virginia, residents near data center zones have complained about the hum of cooling machinery and generators that can be heard day and night. In Georgia, residents living near a large data center reported that their wells dried up or their water became cloudy after construction began.

Purwakarta has settlements and farmland close to the reservoir area. Without safe setback distances and strict oversight, similar problems could occur here: noise, reduced groundwater, and declining water quality.

##### 4. Limited Jobs, and a Process That Is Not Transparent

Data centers are often promised to create many jobs, but in reality this is not the case. Most of the jobs exist only during the construction phase. Once operational, a data center needs only a small number of permanent staff, most of whom require specialized technical skills that local residents may not have.

There are indeed benefits in the form of regional revenue and investment. However, the trade-off is environmental damage and losses for nearby residents, the costs of which are borne for far longer.

In addition, according to West Java Regional Council (DPRD) member Maulana, many council members across commissions were unaware of this project. Yet the permits, environmental impact assessment (AMDAL), and benefit-sharing for a project of this size should have been discussed with the legislature and made open to the public.

#### Conclusion

I acknowledge that Indonesia needs AI data centers so that its data and computing do not depend on foreign countries. However, technological progress must not be paid for with farmers' water, residents' electricity, and the Jatiluhur environment.

Therefore, in my view, this construction must be rejected entirely, not merely postponed. There are two most fundamental reasons:

- **There is no transparency.** A project of this size was started without clear public disclosure; even many regional council members did not know about it. A project that is closed from the start cannot be trusted to be properly supervised.
- **The energy figures do not add up.** The total demand is 640 MW, while the Jatiluhur hydroelectric plant produces only about 186 MW. The first building alone (120 MW) is already equivalent to roughly two-thirds of the dam's capacity, even though that electricity has long been used by the public.

Good development should make nearby residents more prosperous, not leave them bearing the losses.
